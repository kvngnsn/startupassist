# Delaware Corporate Law for Startups — Expert Reference

Last verified: November 2, 2026. Statutory text checked against the official Delaware Code (delcode.delaware.gov), which incorporates the 2026 session laws (85 Del. Laws c. 273 (HB 400) and c. 279 (HB 353)). Pin cites are to Title 8 of the Delaware Code (the General Corporation Law, "DGCL") unless stated otherwise. This file is a reference for Claude as drafter; it is not a template and has no fill-ins.

## 1. Why Delaware

- **Court of Chancery.** A court of equity with no juries, staffed by judges who hear corporate cases full-time, issuing written opinions quickly. Advancement and indemnification suits (§ 145(k)), books-and-records demands (§ 220(c)), stockholder-list disputes (§ 219(b)), ratification and validation of defective acts (§ 205), and summary director elections (§ 211(c), § 223) all go there.
- **Predictability.** Decades of case law on fiduciary duties, takeovers, preferred-versus-common conflicts and financing mechanics; annual statutory amendments drafted by the Corporation Law Council of the Delaware State Bar Association.
- **Investor expectation.** NVCA model documents, Series Seed documents, YC SAFEs, and most institutional fund policies assume a Delaware corporation. Converting later costs more than forming in Delaware at the outset.
- **Flexibility.** Charters can authorize blank-check preferred (§ 151), exculpate directors and officers (§ 102(b)(7)), renounce corporate opportunities (§ 122(17)), and opt out of class votes on authorized-share changes (§ 242(b)(2)); stockholders can act by written consent (§ 228); boards can act by unanimous written consent (§ 141(f)).
- **Responsiveness.** The 2025 amendments (SB 21, 85 Del. Laws c. 6) codified safe harbors for interested and controlling-stockholder transactions and narrowed § 220 books-and-records demands, partly in response to competition from other states for incorporations.
- **Costs.** Delaware franchise tax (Section 7), registered agent fees, and foreign qualification and taxes in the state where the company actually operates. A Delaware corporation headquartered elsewhere pays both.

## 2. Formation steps (summary; the detailed checklist is checklists/formation-checklist.md)

1. Choose and clear the name (Division entity search; trademark search; headquarters-state availability). Optional reservation: 120 days, $75 (§ 102(e); Division fee schedule).
2. Engage a Delaware registered agent (§§ 131–132).
3. File the certificate of incorporation, signed by the incorporator (§§ 101–103). Minimum fee $109 for a one-page filing; certified copy $50 plus $2 per page (§ 391(a)(10)); expedited options.
4. Action of sole incorporator: adopt bylaws, fix board size, elect directors, resign (§§ 107–109).
5. Initial board consent: ratify, elect officers, uncertificated shares, approve forms, value consideration, issue founder stock (§§ 141(f), 142, 151(f), 152–153, 158).
6. Founder RSPAs, technology assignments, CIIAAs; § 151(f) notices; stock ledger (§§ 202, 219(c), 224).
7. 83(b) elections within 30 days of issuance (I.R.C. § 83(b)(2)).
8. EIN; bank account; foreign qualification in the headquarters state; state tax and payroll registrations.
9. Equity plan with a stated reserve; § 409A valuation; first grants.
10. Annual franchise tax report and payment by March 1 every year (§ 502(a)).

No beneficial ownership information (BOI) report is required for a corporation formed in the United States after FinCEN's August 2026 final rule removing domestic entities from the definition of "reporting company." Foreign entities registered to do business in the United States still report; banks still collect beneficial-ownership information under the customer due diligence rule (31 C.F.R. § 1010.230).

## 5. Stockholder agreements (§ 122(18))

- **What it permits.** Notwithstanding § 141(a) (board management), a corporation may contract with current or prospective stockholders (or beneficial owners), in their capacity as such, for minimum consideration determined by the board, and may agree to (a) restrict or prohibit itself from taking specified actions, (b) require approval or consent of specified persons or bodies (including stockholders or directors) before taking specified actions, and (c) covenant that it or specified persons or bodies will take or refrain from specified actions.
- **Limits.** A provision is unenforceable to the extent it is contrary to the charter or would be contrary to Delaware law (other than § 115) if included in the charter. A covenant is not "contrary to law" merely because the DGCL empowers the board to take the action covered.
- **Why it matters.** *Moelis* (Del. Ch. 2024) invalidated pre-approval rights and board-composition covenants in a stockholder agreement as violating § 141(a). The 2024 amendment responds. Investor consent rights in NVCA-style investors' rights and voting agreements now rest on § 122(18). Agreements entered under § 122(18) are "books and records" under § 220(a)(1)h.
- **Drafting.** State that the agreement is entered into under § 122(18); recite the board's determination of consideration; keep protective provisions that must bind future holders in the charter (agreements bind only parties).

## 6. Section 144 (2025) and Section 220 (2025)

**§ 144 safe harbors.**
- *Transactions with directors or officers (not controllers), § 144(a).* No equitable relief or damages against a director or officer because of the interest if: (1) after disclosure of material facts, a majority of the disinterested directors (even if less than a quorum) approve in good faith and without gross negligence — but if a majority of the board is not disinterested, approval must be by a committee of two or more directors each determined by the board to be disinterested; or (2) an informed, uncoerced majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders approves or ratifies; or (3) the transaction is fair to the corporation and its stockholders.
- *Controlling-stockholder transactions other than going-private, § 144(b).* Safe harbor if approved (or recommended) by a committee of at least two disinterested directors to which the board expressly delegated authority to negotiate and to reject; or conditioned from submission on, and approved by, a majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders; or fair.
- *Going-private transactions, § 144(c).* Requires both the committee and the disinterested stockholder approval, or fairness.
- *Definitions, § 144(e).* "Controlling stockholder": majority voting power; contractual right to elect a board majority; or at least one-third voting power plus managerial authority. "Material interest" and "material relationship" are tied to impairing a director's objectivity. Listed-company independence determinations create a heightened presumption of disinterestedness (§ 144(d)(2)). Designation by an interested party is not by itself evidence of interest (§ 144(d)(3)).
- *Controller exculpation, § 144(d)(5).* Controllers are not liable for monetary damages for breaches of fiduciary duty except loyalty breaches, bad faith or knowing violations, and improper personal benefit.
- *Startup applications.* Founder loans, founder-owned IP licenses, founder secondary sales, investor-director-affiliated transactions (for example, a portfolio company buying from another portfolio company), bridge notes from insiders, and down rounds led by existing investors. On a small board where most directors are interested, the committee route may be unavailable; plan for disinterested stockholder approval or build a fairness record (process plus price), recognizing that *In re Trados Inc. S'holder Litig.*, 73 A.3d 17 (Del. Ch. 2013), and *In re Nine Systems Corp. S'holders Litig.* (Del. Ch. 2014) show courts examining both.

**§ 220 books and records.**
- "Books and records" now has a defined list: charter and bylaws (with incorporated documents); stockholder minutes and consents for three years; stockholder communications for three years; board and committee minutes and records of action; materials provided to the board in connection with actions taken; annual financial statements for three years; § 122(18) agreements; director and officer independence questionnaires (§ 220(a)(1)).
- A stockholder must make a written demand under oath, in good faith and for a proper purpose, describing with reasonable particularity the purpose and records sought, and the records must be specifically related to the purpose (§ 220(b)(2)). The corporation may impose reasonable confidentiality restrictions, require incorporation by reference of produced records into any later complaint, and redact unrelated portions (§ 220(b)(3)). Broader production (emails, texts) is available only in limited circumstances under § 220(c)–(d).
- *Practice point.* Formal minutes and board materials are now the core production set; keep them complete and accurate, and avoid conducting board business only over chat or email.

## 7. Franchise tax, annual report, penalties, good standing

**Who and when.** Every Delaware corporation files an annual franchise tax report and pays the tax by **March 1** (§ 502(a)). The report lists the registered office and agent, the nature of the business, the principal place of business (not a registered agent's address), the names and addresses of all directors and of the signing officer, and authorized shares and par value (§ 502(a)(1)–(6)). Filing fee: **$50** for non-exempt domestic corporations (§ 391(a)(18)). Taxpayers owing $5,000 or more pay estimated tax: 40% by June 1, 20% by September 1, 20% by December 1, remainder by March 1 (Division franchise tax page).

**Authorized Shares Method.** 5,000 shares or fewer: $175 (minimum). 5,001–10,000 shares: $250. Each additional 10,000 shares or portion: $85. Maximum $200,000; $250,000 for a Large Corporate Filer (§ 503(c)).

**Assumed Par Value Capital (APVC) Method.**
1. Assumed par = total gross assets ÷ total issued shares (issued shares include treasury shares), carried to six decimal places. "Total gross assets" means all assets net only of allowances for bad debts and accumulated depreciation, depletion and amortization (§ 503(i)); the Division's instructions use the "total assets" on Form 1120, Schedule L, for the fiscal year ending in the calendar year covered by the report.
2. If assumed par is less than stated par, use stated par (§ 503(a)(2)).
3. APVC = the par figure from step 2 × total authorized shares (for each class with par value; no-par shares follow separate rules).
4. Tax = $400 per $1,000,000 of APVC or portion of $1,000,000; minimum $400; same maximums.
5. To use this method the corporation must report its issued shares and gross assets (§ 503(b)); otherwise the State computes the tax under the authorized shares method. Use whichever is lower.

**Worked example — 10,000,000 authorized shares, par $0.00001.**

| Step | Authorized Shares Method | APVC Method (8,000,000 issued; gross assets $250,000) | APVC Method (8,000,000 issued; gross assets $5,000,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | $250 for first 10,000 shares | Assumed par = $250,000 ÷ 8,000,000 = $0.031250 | Assumed par = $5,000,000 ÷ 8,000,000 = $0.625000 |
| Increment | (10,000,000 − 10,000) ÷ 10,000 = 999 × $85 = $84,915 | $0.031250 > $0.00001, so use $0.031250 | $0.625 > $0.00001, so use $0.625 |
| Capital | — | APVC = $0.03125 × 10,000,000 = $312,500 | APVC = $0.625 × 10,000,000 = $6,250,000 |
| Tax | **$85,165** | $312,500 is a portion of one $1M unit → $400 (= minimum) → **$400** | 7 units (6.25 rounded up) × $400 = **$2,800** |
| Plus report fee | $50 | $50 → **$450 total** | $50 → **$2,850 total** |

**Lessons.** (1) Never pay the Authorized Shares figure on the Division's notice without running APVC. (2) Keep authorized shares close to need: APVC multiplies by authorized shares. (3) Low par value keeps APVC at the minimum while assets are small. (4) First-year tax is prorated (§ 503(d)).

**Penalties and consequences.** Late or missing report: $200 penalty (§ 502(c)). Unpaid tax: interest at 1.5% per month or portion (§ 504(c)). No certificate of good standing while tax or report is outstanding (§ 502(f)). If tax is unpaid or the report unfiled for one year, the charter becomes void (§ 510), after a notice the Secretary of State sends by November 30; revival requires a certificate of revival and payment of back taxes, penalties and interest. A voided corporation's acts in the interim create uncertainty that revival (8 Del. C. § 312) generally cures, but avoid it.

**Good standing.** Investors, banks, acquirers and other states (for foreign qualification) require a Delaware certificate of good standing, which issues only if the corporation has filed its reports, paid its taxes, and maintains a registered agent.

## 8. Exculpation and indemnification

- **Exculpation (§ 102(b)(7))** limits monetary damages for fiduciary breaches. It does not prevent injunctions, does not cover loyalty or bad-faith claims, does not cover directors' § 174 liability, and does not cover officers in derivative suits. Plaintiffs therefore plead loyalty, bad faith or *Caremark* oversight claims to survive dismissal against exculpated directors.
- **Indemnification (§ 145).** Permissive for third-party suits (expenses, judgments, fines, settlements) if the person acted in good faith and reasonably believed the conduct was in or not opposed to the corporation's best interests (and, in criminal matters, had no reasonable cause to believe it unlawful) (§ 145(a)); for derivative suits, expenses only, and none if adjudged liable to the corporation absent court approval (§ 145(b)); mandatory for present or former directors and § 3114(b) officers successful "on the merits or otherwise" (§ 145(c)); determinations by disinterested directors, a committee, independent counsel, or stockholders (§ 145(d)); advancement upon an undertaking (§ 145(e)); non-exclusive, and charter or bylaw rights cannot be eliminated after the relevant act unless the provision then in effect allowed it (§ 145(f), responding to *Schoon v. Troy Corp.*, 948 A.2d 1157 (Del. Ch. 2008)); insurance including captive insurance (§ 145(g)); Court of Chancery exclusive jurisdiction and summary advancement proceedings (§ 145(k)).
- **Market structure.** Mandatory indemnification and advancement in bylaws plus individual indemnification agreements plus D&O insurance. Fund-indemnitor ("indemnitor of first resort") provisions make the company primary to venture funds that also indemnify their designees. They respond to *Levy v. HLI Operating Co.*, 924 A.2d 210 (Del. Ch. 2007), which held that, absent an agreement setting priority, a fund and the portfolio company share the indemnity obligation and the paying indemnitor may seek contribution from the other.
- **Advancement is separate.** Courts enforce advancement promptly, without deciding the merits (*Homestore, Inc. v. Tafeen*, 888 A.2d 204 (Del. 2005)).

## 9. Forum selection; defective acts (§§ 204–205)

**Forum selection.** Chancery-forum provisions for internal corporate claims are valid in charters and bylaws (§ 115; *Boilermakers Local 154 Ret. Fund v. Chevron Corp.*, 73 A.3d 934 (Del. Ch. 2013)). Federal-forum provisions for Securities Act claims are facially valid (*Salzberg*). Exchange Act claims are exclusively federal anyway. § 115(c) (2025) permits channeling of non-internal stockholder claims if a Delaware court remains available. Fee-shifting is prohibited (§§ 102(f), 109(b)).

**Defective corporate acts — summary** (the ratification template is in the governance family).
- *What can be fixed.* A "defective corporate act" is an overissue, a void or voidable election or appointment of directors, or any act within the corporation's power that is void or voidable due to a "failure of authorization" — noncompliance with the DGCL, charter, bylaws, a plan or agreement, or the absence of required board or officer approval (§ 204(h)(1)–(2)). Common startup examples: founder stock issued before the board was constituted or without board approval; shares issued in excess of authorized shares; option grants beyond the plan reserve or without a valid board approval; directors never validly elected because no incorporator action was signed.
- *Why it matters.* Delaware treats stock issued without proper authorization as void, not merely voidable (*STAAR Surgical Co. v. Waggoner*, 588 A.2d 1130 (Del. 1991); *Nevins v. Bryan*, 885 A.2d 233 (Del. Ch. 2005)); equitable defenses cannot cure void acts (*Klaassen v. Allegro Dev. Corp.*, 106 A.3d 1035 (Del. 2014), distinguishing void from voidable).
- *§ 204 procedure.* Board resolutions identifying each defective act, its date, the nature of the failure of authorization, and approving ratification (§ 204(b)(1)); special procedure to ratify the election of the initial board when no valid incorporator action exists, adopted by a majority of the persons then exercising the powers of directors and naming who first acted as the initial board and when (§ 204(b)(2)); stockholder approval at the vote that would have been required, with 20 days' meeting notice where a meeting is used (§ 204(c)–(d)); a certificate of validation if the act required a filing (§ 204(e)); prompt notice to holders of valid and putative stock (§ 204(g)). Ratified acts are effective retroactively to their original date ("validation effective time"), unless a court determines otherwise.
- *§ 205.* The Court of Chancery may determine the validity of any corporate act or stock, validate defective acts, and modify § 204 procedures; claims that a § 204 ratification is ineffective must be brought within 120 days after the later of the validation effective time and notice (§ 205(f)).
- *What not to do.* Backdate consents; have founders sign a stockholder consent "electing" the board that has already been acting; re-issue stock "as of" an earlier date. Each creates a record that diligence will find and that does not cure the original defect.

## 10. Fiduciary duty basics for startup boards

- **Duty of care.** Directors must act on an informed basis, considering all material information reasonably available (*Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985)). Typically exculpated for damages (§ 102(b)(7)) but still matters for injunctions and officer derivative claims.
- **Duty of loyalty**, including good faith and oversight. Directors may not prefer personal interests over the corporation's. Oversight (*Caremark*) requires a good-faith effort to implement and monitor reporting systems for mission-critical risks (*In re Caremark Int'l Inc. Derivative Litig.*, 698 A.2d 959 (Del. Ch. 1996); *Marchand v. Barnhill*, 212 A.3d 805 (Del. 2019)). Loyalty claims are not exculpable.
- **Standards of review.** Business judgment rule (default; disinterested, informed, good-faith decisions are not second-guessed); enhanced scrutiny for defensive measures and sales of control (*Unocal*, *Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.*, 506 A.2d 173 (Del. 1986)); entire fairness (fair dealing and fair price) where a majority of the board is interested or a controller stands on both sides (*Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983)). Cleansing devices: fully informed, uncoerced disinterested stockholder approval (*Corwin v. KKR Fin. Holdings LLC*, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015)); for controller transactions, the dual protections of an empowered independent committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote (*Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014)) — now largely superseded for covered transactions by the § 144(b)–(c) statutory safe harbors.
- **Common versus preferred.** Directors' fiduciary duties run to the corporation and its residual claimants — the common stockholders. Preferred stockholders' special rights are contractual; directors may not favor the preferred's contractual interests over the common where the two diverge (*In re Trados*, 73 A.3d 17 (Del. Ch. 2013); *Frederick Hsu Living Tr. v. ODN Holding Corp.* (Del. Ch. 2017)). Investor-designated directors must recognize that dual role. In an exit where the preferred take all or most proceeds, build a record: independent directors or committee, fairness analysis, consideration of alternatives, and disclosure.
- **Down rounds and insider financings.** Insider-led financings at low valuations are classic entire-fairness cases (*In re Nine Systems* found a fair price but unfair process). Use a rights offering to all holders, a disinterested committee or disinterested stockholder approval under § 144, and a market check where feasible.
- **Practical rules for a startup board.** Hold real meetings for significant decisions; distribute materials in advance; keep accurate minutes (now central to § 220); disclose conflicts and recuse; get valuations for equity grants (§ 409A) and for related-party transactions; never let the cap table, charter and board approvals drift apart.
