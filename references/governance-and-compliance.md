# Governance and Compliance Reference

Expert reference for Claude when drafting or reviewing board and stockholder actions, advising on fiduciary process, fixing defective corporate acts, and running the annual compliance cycle of a venture-backed Delaware C corporation. Law stated as of **November 2, 2026**. Primary sources are listed at the end; where a point rests on secondary sources or could not be confirmed, it says so.

**Templates this reference supports:** templates/governance/ (board-consent-resolution-library, board-meeting-materials, stockholder-consent-and-notice, stockholder-meeting-materials, secretarys-certificate, certificate-of-amendment, section-204-ratification, director-agreement) and templates/compliance/ (delaware-franchise-tax-and-annual-report, foreign-qualification-guide, entity-conversion-guide, dissolution-guide), plus checklists/annual-compliance-calendar.md.

Wertheimer, 2002 WL 205684 (Del. Ch. Jan. 25, 2002); such action is voidable, not void, and equitable defenses such as acquiescence can apply (Klaassen v. Allegro Dev. Corp., 106 A.3d 1035 (Del. 2014)).

### 1.2 Unanimous written consent (§ 141(f))

- Any action that could be taken at a board or committee meeting may be taken without a meeting if **all members** consent in writing or by electronic transmission, unless the charter or bylaws restrict it. "All members" means every director then in office; one holdout defeats the consent.
- The consent may be documented, signed, and delivered in any manner permitted by § 116 (electronic signatures and delivery to a designated information processing system).
- **Future effectiveness:** a person may provide that a consent becomes effective at a future time (including on the happening of an event) no later than 60 days after the instruction is given; it counts only if the person is a director at that time and has not revoked it. Consents are revocable before they become effective.
- After the action is taken, the consent is filed with the minutes, in the same paper or electronic form as the minutes.
- **No backdating.** Action occurs when the last required consent becomes effective. For equity awards, the grant date for ISO and § 409A purposes is the date the corporate action is completed (Treas. Reg. § 1.421-1(c); § 1.409A-1(b)(5)(vi)(B)). The skill's consent shell states a definite future Effective Time and fails if a signature is missing at that time.

### 1.3 Electronic documents and signatures (§ 116)

- Any act governed by the DGCL, the charter, or the bylaws may be documented in an electronic transmission; signatures may be manual, facsimile, conformed, or electronic; delivery to an information processing system the recipient designated counts even if no one reads it (§ 116(a)).
- **Exclusions (§ 116(b)):** documents filed with the Secretary of State, the Register in Chancery, or a court; the stock ledger; stock certificates; documents "expressly referenced as a notice (or waiver of notice)"; ballots at stockholder meetings; and acts under § 280 and subchapters III, XIII, and XVI. These are governed by their own provisions (for example, § 229 permits waivers by electronic transmission, and § 232 governs notices).
- A charter or bylaw can limit § 116 only by expressly restricting electronic transmission or signatures.

### 1.4 Officers and delegation

- Officers and their titles and duties are set by the bylaws or board resolution; one person may hold several offices (§ 142(a)); officers hold office until a successor is chosen or until resignation or removal; an officer may resign at any time on written notice (§ 142(b)).
- **Delegation of stock and option issuance:** the board may by resolution delegate to "a person or body" authority to issue stock (§ 152(b)) or rights and options (§ 157(c)) if the resolution fixes (i) the maximum number of shares, (ii) the time period, and (iii) the minimum consideration, and does not permit self-issuance. The three required terms may not depend on a determination by the delegate (§§ 152(c), 157(d)).
- **Consideration:** the board determines the form of consideration for stock (cash, any tangible or intangible property, or any benefit to the corporation) (§ 152(a)); par-value shares require consideration of at least par (§ 153(a)); the board's judgment of value is conclusive absent actual fraud, and stock is fully paid and nonassessable on receipt of the consideration (§ 152(d)).

### 2.2 Written consent (§ 228)

- Permitted unless the charter prohibits it. Needs holders of outstanding stock having at least the minimum votes needed at a meeting at which **all shares entitled to vote were present and voted**.
- **60-day window:** consents signed by enough holders must be delivered within 60 days of the first date a consent is delivered. Future-effective consents up to 60 days are allowed and revocable before effectiveness unless otherwise provided. A signer who is not a holder of record when signing counts only if a holder of record on the record date (§ 228(c)).
- **Delivery (§ 228(d)):** to the principal place of business; to the officer or agent with custody of the minute book; to the registered office by hand or certified or registered mail; or under § 116 to an information processing system the corporation designates, with information establishing the date of delivery and the signer's identity. An electronic consent delivered by the first three methods must be reproduced and delivered on paper.
- **Record date (§ 213(b)):** the board may fix a date not earlier than its resolution and not more than 10 days after it; otherwise, the first delivery date of a signed consent (no prior board action required) or the close of business on the day the board takes required prior action.
- **Notice (§ 228(e)):** prompt notice to non-consenting holders who would have been entitled to notice of a meeting if the record date for the meeting were the consent record date; a certificate filed for the action recites that consent was given under § 228.

### 2.3 Proxies (§ 212)

One vote per share unless the charter provides otherwise; proxies valid for three years unless they provide a longer period; may be granted by signed document, electronic transmission with authenticating information, or under § 116 with identifying information; irrevocable only if stated and coupled with an interest sufficient in law (the basis for voting-agreement proxies).

### 2.4 Notice by electronic transmission (§ 232)

- Notice may be given by mail (given when deposited), courier (earlier of receipt or delivery to the address), or **email** (given when directed to the stockholder's email address) unless the stockholder has objected in writing or by electronic transmission. Email notice must include "a prominent legend that the communication is an important notice regarding the corporation" (§ 232(a)).
- Other forms of electronic transmission (posting with separate notice, other channels) require the stockholder's revocable consent (§ 232(b)–(c)).
- Electronic notice stops after two consecutive undeliverable notices become known to the Secretary or person responsible for notice (§ 232(e)).
- A Secretary's or transfer agent's affidavit of notice is prima facie evidence absent fraud (§ 232(f)).

### 2.5 Voting thresholds

## 3. Fiduciary duties

### 3.1 The basics

- **Care:** act on an informed basis after considering all material information reasonably available. Standard of liability: gross negligence. The charter may eliminate monetary damages for directors' care breaches and, since the 2022 amendment to § 102(b)(7), for officers' care breaches in direct (not derivative) claims; exculpation never covers loyalty breaches, bad faith, intentional misconduct, knowing violations of law, improper personal benefit, or (for directors) unlawful dividends under § 174.
- **Loyalty (including good faith):** no self-dealing, no usurping corporate opportunities, no conscious disregard of known duties.
- **Standards of review:** business judgment rule (presumption of informed, good-faith, disinterested action); enhanced scrutiny (defensive measures and sales of control); entire fairness (fair dealing and fair price, when a majority of the board is conflicted or a controller stands on both sides, unless a safe harbor applies).
- **Disclosure:** when directors seek stockholder action, they must disclose fully and fairly all material information within the board's control (Malone v. Brincat, 722 A.2d 5 (Del. 1998)). Informed, uncoerced approval by disinterested stockholders can cleanse transactions not involving a controller (Corwin v. KKR Fin. Holdings LLC, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015)); for controller transactions, see § 144(b)–(c) below.

### 3.2 Oversight (Caremark) and officers

- Directors must make a good-faith effort to ensure a reasonable information and reporting system exists, and must not consciously disregard red flags (In re Caremark Int'l Inc. Derivative Litig., 698 A.2d 959 (Del. Ch. 1996); Stone v. Ritter, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006)). For "mission critical" compliance risks, the board itself must have a reporting system (Marchand v. Barnhill, 212 A.3d 805 (Del. 2019)).
- **Officers** owe a duty of oversight within their areas of responsibility, including a duty to report red flags upward; a sexual-harassment red-flag claim against a senior officer survived dismissal in In re McDonald's Corp. Stockholder Derivative Litigation, 289 A.3d 343 (Del. Ch. 2023). Oversight liability requires bad faith, so § 102(b)(7) exculpation does not bar it.
- Startup application: the board should receive regular reporting on the risks that could sink the company (product safety, data privacy and security, regulatory licenses, financial controls, cash runway), and minutes should show it.

### 3.3 Venture-backed boards: preferred versus common

- Preferred stock's special rights are contractual. Where the board exercises discretion beyond those contractual rights, Delaware generally expects directors to maximize value for the common stockholders as residual claimants (In re Trados Inc. S'holder Litig., 73 A.3d 17 (Del. Ch. 2013): process unfair where VC-designated directors pursued a sale triggering the preference and a management carve-out, but the deal was entirely fair because the common had no value to lose; Frederick Hsu Living Trust v. ODN Holding Corp., 2017 WL 1437308 (Del. Ch. Apr. 14, 2017): redemption context).
- VC-designated directors are "dual fiduciaries" (to the fund and to the company); when their fund's interests diverge from the common's (insider-led financings, sales near the preference, redemptions, pay-to-play), they are not disinterested.
- Process remedies: a committee of disinterested directors (§ 144) with a real mandate and its own advisors; information on alternatives; attention to the common recorded in minutes; for insider financings, a rights offering to all qualifying holders; for sales, a fairness opinion where the stakes justify it. See templates/governance/board-consent-resolution-library.md, Modules M and N.

### 3.4 Section 144 after SB 21 (2025)

SB 21 (85 Del. Laws c. 6) was approved March 25, 2025, took effect on enactment, and applies to all acts and transactions whether before or after enactment, except for actions completed or pending, and § 220 demands made, on or before February 17, 2025 (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 3). The Delaware Supreme Court, answering questions certified by the Court of Chancery, upheld the § 144 amendments and their retroactive application against constitutional challenge in Rutledge v. Clearway Energy Group LLC (Del. Feb. 27, 2026); the § 220 amendments were not before the court.

| Transaction | Safe harbor (any one suffices, except going-private) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Director or officer interested transaction (not a controlling-stockholder transaction) | (1) Material facts disclosed to or known by all board or committee members, and approved in good faith and without gross negligence by a majority of the disinterested directors then serving (even if less than a quorum); if a majority of the board is not disinterested, approval by a committee of 2+ directors each determined by the board to be disinterested; (2) approved or ratified by an informed, uncoerced vote of a majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders; or (3) fair to the corporation and its stockholders | § 144(a) |
| Controlling-stockholder transaction (not going-private) | (1) Approved (or recommended) in good faith and without gross negligence by a majority of the disinterested directors on a committee of 2+ disinterested directors to which the board expressly delegated authority to negotiate (or oversee negotiation of) and to reject the transaction, after disclosure of material facts; (2) conditioned, when submitted to stockholders, on disinterested stockholder approval, and approved by an informed, uncoerced majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders; or (3) fair | § 144(b) |
| Going-private controlling-stockholder transaction | Both the committee approval in § 144(b)(1) and the stockholder approval in § 144(b)(2), or fairness | § 144(c) |

Key definitions (§ 144(e)): **controlling stockholder** (with affiliates and associates) — majority voting power; or a contractual or other right to elect a board majority; or at least one-third of the voting power plus power to exercise managerial authority; **control group** — two or more non-controllers that by agreement, arrangement, or understanding constitute a controller; **disinterested director** — not a party to the transaction and without a material interest in it or a material relationship with a person who has one; **material interest** and **material relationship** — tied to impairment of the director's objectivity (for directors) or materiality (for others); **going-private transaction** for a private company — a controller transaction in which all or substantially all of the disinterested stockholders' shares are cancelled, converted, purchased, or cease to be outstanding.

Other rules: interested directors count toward quorum (§ 144(d)(1)); listed-company directors determined independent under exchange rules are presumed disinterested (§ 144(d)(2)); being designated or nominated by an interested person is not, alone, evidence of interest (§ 144(d)(3)); controllers are liable for monetary damages only for loyalty breaches, bad faith, intentional misconduct, knowing violations, or improper personal benefit (§ 144(d)(5)); the safe harbors do not limit claims that an act was not authorized in compliance with the DGCL, charter, or bylaws, injunctive review of defensive devices, or aiding-and-abetting claims (§ 144(d)(6)).

**Practical checklist for a § 144(a)(1) approval:** (1) identify every interested director and the nature of the interest; (2) board determination, director by director, that the others are disinterested; (3) if the disinterested directors are not a majority of the board, form a committee of at least two disinterested directors first; (4) written disclosure of the material facts, including the interested party's role in initiating and negotiating; (5) deliberation without the interested director; (6) vote of a majority of the disinterested directors then serving; (7) minutes or consent recitals documenting all of it. Venture-backed patterns that need this: insider bridge rounds, investor-affiliated vendor deals, loans from founders or investors, secondary purchases by insiders, and director or officer compensation.

### 3.5 Stockholder agreements (§ 122(18), 2024)

After West Palm Beach Firefighters' Pension Fund v. Moelis & Co., 311 A.3d 809 (Del. Ch. 2024), the 2024 amendments added § 122(18), authorizing the corporation, notwithstanding § 141(a), to contract with current or prospective stockholders (or beneficial owners), in their capacity as such and for minimum consideration the board determines, to restrict or prohibit specified corporate actions, to require the approval or consent of specified persons or bodies before taking them, or to covenant that specified actions will or will not be taken. A provision is unenforceable against the corporation to the extent it is contrary to the charter or would be contrary to Delaware law (other than § 115) if it were included in the charter. Venture investors' rights agreements and voting agreements that give investors consent rights are within this power; such agreements are "books and records" under § 220(a)(1)h. Directors' fiduciary duties continue to apply when the board decides whether to enter into such an agreement.

### 3.6 Director compensation

The board may fix director compensation unless the charter or bylaws restrict it (§ 141(h)). Self-set discretionary director awards are reviewed for entire fairness unless stockholders approved the specific awards or the plan is self-executing (In re Investors Bancorp, Inc. Stockholder Litigation, 177 A.3d 1208 (Del. 2017)). See templates/governance/director-agreement.md.

## 4. Books and records: § 220 after SB 21

- **Defined "books and records" (§ 220(a)(1)):** the charter and bylaws (with incorporated agreements); stockholder meeting minutes and stockholder consents for the past 3 years; communications to stockholders generally for the past 3 years; minutes of board and committee meetings and records of their actions; materials provided to the board or committees in connection with their actions; annual financial statements for the past 3 years; § 122(18) agreements; and director and officer independence questionnaires.
- **Demand requirements (§ 220(b)):** written demand under oath, in good faith and for a proper purpose (reasonably related to the holder's interest as a stockholder), describing with reasonable particularity the purpose and the records sought; the records must be specifically related to the purpose. Beneficial owners must attach documentary evidence of ownership.
- **Company protections:** reasonable confidentiality, use, and distribution restrictions; a condition that the produced information is deemed incorporated by reference in any related complaint; redaction of portions not specifically related to the purpose (§ 220(b)(3)).
- **Court limits:** a court may order only the defined books and records (§ 220(e)), except (1) "functional equivalents" if the corporation lacks board or stockholder minutes, annual financial statements, or (for listed companies) independence questionnaires (§ 220(f)), and (2) other specific records only on a showing of compelling need and clear and convincing evidence that the records are necessary and essential to the proper purpose (§ 220(g)).
- **Timing:** a stockholder may sue if the corporation refuses or fails to reply within 5 business days (§ 220(c)).
- **Directors:** may inspect records for purposes reasonably related to the director's position; the corporation bears the burden of showing an improper purpose (§ 220(d)).
- **Lesson for startups:** keep real minutes, consents, and board decks. Without minutes, emails and messages become the "functional equivalent."

## 5. Defective corporate acts: §§ 204 and 205

- Void acts (stock issued in violation of the charter or the DGCL) cannot be ratified at common law (STAAR Surgical Co. v. Waggoner, 588 A.2d 1130 (Del. 1991)). § 204 lets the board ratify a "defective corporate act" (an overissue; a void or voidable director election; or any act within corporate power that is void or voidable due to a "failure of authorization") with retroactive effect; § 205 lets the Court of Chancery validate or decide validity.
- **Board resolutions must state (§ 204(b)(1)):** the act; its date; for putative stock, the number and type of shares and dates purportedly issued; the nature of the failure of authorization; and that the board approves ratification. Initial-board elections are ratified under § 204(b)(2) by a majority of persons exercising director powers.
- **Stockholder approval (§ 204(c)–(d))** if any law, charter or bylaw provision, plan, or agreement would have required it (at the time of the act or of ratification) or a § 203 failure is involved, unless no valid stock is outstanding and entitled to vote. Putative stock does not vote or count for quorum. Meeting notice: at least 20 days, to holders of valid and putative stock (voting or not) and holders as of the time of the act.
- **Certificate of validation (§ 204(e))** when the act would have required a § 103 filing; multiple overissues may share one, effective as of the first overissue. Fee (schedule revised August 1, 2026): $2,599 for one page, more if increasing authorized stock; preclearance $1,500; no expedited service. A certificate of validation does not reduce franchise tax for prior periods, and additional tax and interest are computed at current rates (§ 503(e)).
- **Notice (§ 204(g))** promptly to holders of valid and putative stock as of the adoption date (or a date within 60 days after it) and as of the time of each act; may be combined with the § 228(e) notice. **120-day bar** on challenges from the later of the validation effective time and notice, except claims that the ratification itself was not done properly and claims by persons who should have received notice and did not (§ 205(f)).

## 7. Annual compliance

The month-by-month calendar is checklists/annual-compliance-calendar.md. This section explains each item.

### 7.1 Delaware

- **Annual Franchise Tax Report and franchise tax due March 1** for the prior calendar year (§§ 502(a), 504(a)); report fee $50 (§ 391(a)(18)).
- **Authorized Shares Method:** $175 for 5,000 shares or fewer; $250 for 5,001–10,000; $85 for each additional 10,000 shares or part; maximum $200,000 (§ 503(a)(1), (c)).
- **Assumed Par Value Capital Method:** assumed par = total gross assets (Form 1120 Schedule L) ÷ total issued shares; APVC = assumed par × authorized shares (or par value × shares, for classes whose par exceeds the assumed par); tax $400 per $1,000,000 of APVC or part thereof; minimum $400; maximum $200,000 (§ 503(a)(2), (c), (i)). The corporation pays the lesser method but must report issued shares and gross assets to get APVC (§ 503(b)).
- **Large corporate filers:** $250,000 (§ 503(c)).
- **Estimated payments** when the tax is $5,000 or more: 40% June 1, 20% September 1, 20% December 1, balance March 1 (§ 504(a)).
- **Penalties:** $200 for a late or incomplete report (§ 502(c)); interest at 1.5% per month (§ 504(c)); no good standing while delinquent (§ 502(f)); charter void after one year of nonpayment or non-filing (§ 510); revival under § 312.
- **Registered agent** fees are due annually to the commercial agent; a lapse can leave no one to receive process.
- Template and worked examples: templates/compliance/delaware-franchise-tax-and-annual-report.md; calculator: `scripts/franchise_tax.py`.

### 7.2 Foreign qualification renewals

California Statement of Information annually (and within 90 days after qualifying), $25, $250 penalty; New York biennial statement, $9; Texas franchise tax report or Public Information Report by May 15; Washington annual report ($70) by the end of the registration month; Massachusetts annual report within 2½ months after fiscal year end; Illinois annual report and franchise tax before the anniversary month; Colorado periodic report ($25); Florida annual report ($150) January 1–May 1; Georgia annual registration ($60) January 1–April 1. Details and sources: templates/compliance/foreign-qualification-guide.md.

### 7.3 Federal income tax

- **Form 1120** due the 15th day of the 4th month after year end (April 15 for calendar years) (I.R.C. § 6072(a)); Form 7004 gives an automatic 6-month extension to file, not to pay (Treas. Reg. § 1.6081-3).
- **Estimated tax** in four installments due the 15th day of the 4th, 6th, 9th, and 12th months (I.R.C. § 6655(c)); underpayment additions apply.
- **Form 5472** for a U.S. corporation 25% or more foreign-owned (one foreign person owning at least 25% of vote or value), attached to Form 1120; penalty $25,000 per failure plus $25,000 per 30 days of continued failure after 90 days from IRS notice (I.R.C. § 6038A).
- **FBAR (FinCEN Form 114)** if foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000 in aggregate at any time: due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.
- **Form 966** within 30 days after adopting a plan or resolution to dissolve or liquidate (I.R.C. § 6043(a)).

### 7.4 State income and franchise taxes

- **California:** 8.84% of net income; $800 minimum franchise tax (not owed for the first taxable year of a corporation that incorporates or qualifies on or after January 1, 2000) (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code §§ 23151, 23153); estimated payments 30%/40%/0%/30% on the 15th day of months 4, 6, 9, and 12 (§ 19025); Form 100 due the 15th day of month 4 with an automatic 7-month extension to file. A Delaware corporation with California founders or employees is "doing business" in California.
- **New York:** Article 9-A (Form CT-3); economic nexus at $1,283,000 of New York receipts for tax years 2024–2026; fixed dollar minimum based on receipts; New York City tax separately.
- **Texas:** franchise (margin) tax; no tax due at or below $2,650,000 of revenue for 2026 and 2027 reports, but the Public Information Report or Ownership Information Report is still due May 15.
- **Massachusetts:** corporate excise minimum $456. **Washington:** no corporate income tax; B&O gross receipts tax above $100,000 of Washington receipts.

### 7.6 Sales tax after Wayfair

South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc., 585 U.S. 162 (2018), allows states to require remote sellers to collect sales tax based on economic presence. Thresholds vary: California applies a $500,000 threshold of sales of tangible personal property delivered into California (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code § 6203); many states use $100,000, and several have dropped transaction-count tests (Indiana from January 1, 2024; Utah from July 1, 2025; Illinois from January 1, 2026). Whether software-as-a-service or digital products are taxable varies widely (taxable in some states, such as Washington and New York; generally not in California). Review nexus at least annually and whenever sales into a state grow.

> **Verify:** State thresholds and SaaS taxability change frequently; confirm with each state's revenue department or a current multistate survey before advising.

### 7.7 Research and experimental expenditures and the payroll R&D credit after P.L. 119-21

- **New I.R.C. § 174A:** domestic research or experimental expenditures paid or incurred in tax years beginning after December 31, 2024 are currently deductible; software development is treated as R&E; a taxpayer may instead elect to capitalize and amortize over not less than 60 months. Foreign research remains capitalized and amortized over 15 years under § 174 (P.L. 119-21 § 70302).
- **Catch-up for 2022–2024:** any taxpayer may elect to deduct remaining unamortized domestic R&E from tax years beginning in 2022–2024 in full in the first tax year beginning after December 31, 2024 (2025 for calendar-year companies) or ratably over that year and the next (2025 and 2026). Rev. Proc. 2025-28 gives the procedures.
- **Small-business retroactive election:** taxpayers meeting the § 448(c) gross receipts test (average annual gross receipts of $31,000,000 or less for 2025) could apply § 174A retroactively to tax years beginning after December 31, 2021 by amended returns. Under Rev. Proc. 2025-28, an amended-return election was timely only if filed by the earlier of July 6, 2026 or the § 6511 refund-claim deadline. **That deadline has passed** as of this reference's date; do not advise clients that it is still available absent new guidance.
- **§ 280C(c):** for tax years beginning after 2024, domestic R&E deductions are reduced by the amount of the research credit unless the taxpayer makes the reduced-credit election on a timely filed original return.
- **§ 41(h) payroll tax election (unchanged by P.L. 119-21):** a "qualified small business" (gross receipts under $5,000,000 for the year and no gross receipts in any year before the 5-taxable-year period ending with the current year) may elect to apply up to $500,000 of research credit per year against payroll taxes: $250,000 against the employer's share of Social Security (OASDI) tax and, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2022, an additional $250,000 against the employer's Medicare tax (I.R.C. §§ 41(h)(4)(B), 3111(f)). Available for at most 5 years. Elected on Form 6765 with a timely filed original return, including extensions (not on an amended return); claimed on Form 8974 with Form 941 beginning with the first calendar quarter that begins after the income tax return is filed. Example: a calendar-2025 return filed on October 15, 2026 starts the credit in the first quarter of 2027.

### 7.8 Securities compliance

- **Form D:** within 15 calendar days after the first sale in a Regulation D offering (17 C.F.R. § 230.503(a)(1)); an annual amendment on or before the first anniversary of the most recent filing while the offering continues (§ 230.503(a)(3)(iii)); amendments for material changes. State notice filings follow each state's rules (references/securities-law.md).
- **Rule 701:** compensatory issuances in any 12-month period may not exceed the greatest of $1,000,000, 15% of total assets, or 15% of the outstanding class (17 C.F.R. § 230.701(d)); if sales in a 12-month period exceed $10,000,000, the company must deliver additional disclosure, including financial statements dated no more than 180 days before the sale (§ 230.701(e)). Track grants and exercises on a rolling basis.
- **California § 25102(o)** and **§ 25102(f)** notices as applicable.

### 7.9 Section 409A valuations

An independent appraisal is presumed reasonable if it is as of a date no more than 12 months before the grant, absent material information arising after it (Treas. Reg. § 1.409A-1(b)(5)(iv)(B)(2)(i)); the presumption for an illiquid startup's written valuation by a qualified person (§ 1.409A-1(b)(5)(iv)(B)(2)(iii)) is unavailable if a change in control is reasonably anticipated within 90 days or an IPO within 180 days. Refresh at least every 12 months and after any priced round, significant revenue change, or acquisition discussion.

### 7.10 Other recurring items

- **D&O and other insurance** renewals; confirm the D&O policy lists current directors and officers.
- **Equity plan housekeeping:** reserve remaining; ISO $100,000 first-exercisable limit (I.R.C. § 422(d)); plan expiration (ISOs must be granted within 10 years after the earlier of plan adoption or stockholder approval, I.R.C. § 422(b)(2)); post-termination exercise deadlines.
- **Board and stockholder housekeeping:** annual consent electing directors (§ 211(b)); D&O questionnaires; minutes up to date; cap table reconciled to the stock ledger.
- **Employment reporting:** EEO-1 for employers with 100 or more employees (timing set annually by the EEOC); California pay data report for private employers with 100 or more employees (due annually in May).

> **Verify:** Confirm the EEO-1 collection window and the California pay data reporting due date for the current year (the California report has been due on the second Wednesday of May under Gov. Code § 12999).

## 8. Beneficial ownership reporting after the 2026 FinCEN rule

- FinCEN's final rule announced August 11, 2026 (published and effective August 14, 2026, 91 Fed. Reg. 52508) permanently removes all entities created in the United States from the Corporate Transparency Act's "reporting company" definition. A Delaware corporation files no beneficial ownership information (BOI) report, initial or updated. FinCEN has stated that it will delete previously reported BOI of U.S. persons in a one-time sweep (it will post a notice when done but will not confirm deletion to individual filers).
- Foreign entities registered to do business in the United States remain reporting companies and report their non-U.S. beneficial owners.
- Banks and other covered financial institutions still collect beneficial-ownership information from legal-entity customers under the Customer Due Diligence Rule (31 C.F.R. § 1010.230: each 25% equity owner and one control person). Expect those requests at account opening and periodically.
- New York's LLC Transparency Act (effective January 1, 2026) applies to limited liability companies formed or registered in New York, not to corporations.
- Older templates, engagement letters, and investor questionnaires that reference CTA filings should be updated.
