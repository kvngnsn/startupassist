## About this template

**What it does.** The CIIAA (also called a PIIA, or "Employee Proprietary Information and Inventions Agreement") is the single most important document in a startup's intellectual-property chain of title. It (1) presently assigns to the Company every invention and work the employee creates within the scope of the relationship, (2) imposes confidentiality on Company information, (3) captures and limits the employee's pre-existing inventions, (4) gives the Company the cooperation and power of attorney needed to file and record patents and copyrights, and (5) delivers the legally required notices (state invention-assignment exclusions and the DTSA immunity notice). Every institutional investor's diligence request list asks for a signed CIIAA from every current and former employee and contractor; missing or defective CIIAAs are among the most common closing conditions in Series Seed and Series A financings.

**Where it sits.** The offer letter makes signing the CIIAA a condition of employment. The employee signs it on or before the first day of work. If the employee began work earlier (common for founders and first hires), the agreement covers the whole relationship from the original start date (Section 1.9) and should be supported by fresh consideration for mid-employment signers in states that require it (see "Consideration" below). If a founder's pre-incorporation work is involved, also use the founders' technology assignment, and list carve-outs carefully on Exhibit A.

**Why "hereby assigns."** Inventors own their inventions until they assign them, and mere employment does not transfer title (Stanford v. Roche, 563 U.S. 776, 786 (2011)). An "agree to assign" clause is a promise to assign in the future, while "do hereby assign" presently assigns future inventions as soon as they exist (Stanford v. Roche, 583 F.3d 832, 841–42 (Fed. Cir. 2009)). Section 4.2 uses "hereby assigns, and agrees to assign" so that the present assignment operates automatically and the promise backs it up. Copyright in employee works created within the scope of employment vests in the employer as a "work made for hire" (17 U.S.C. §§ 101, 201(b)), but scope-of-employment disputes are common (side projects, work done before the start date, work done off-hours), so the present assignment covers whatever the work-for-hire doctrine misses.

**State invention-assignment statutes.** Eleven states limit or define what an employer may require an employee to assign. Five of them require the employer to give the employee **written notice** of the limit "at the time the agreement is made": California (Lab. Code § 2872), Illinois (765 ILCS 1060/2(3)), Kansas (K.S.A. 44-130(c)), Minnesota (Minn. Stat. § 181.78, subd. 3), and Washington (RCW 49.44.140(3)). Delaware (19 Del. C. § 805), New Jersey (N.J.S.A. 34:1B-265), New York (Lab. Law § 203-f, effective Sept. 15, 2023), North Carolina (G.S. §§ 66-57.1, 66-57.2), and Utah (Utah Code § 34-39-3) limit assignment but do not require notice. Nevada (NRS 600.500) runs the other way: absent a written agreement to the contrary, the employer owns patentable inventions and trade secrets the employee develops in the course and scope of employment that relate directly to that work. Exhibit B delivers every required notice, quotes the statutes verbatim, and is attached for every employee regardless of state (it costs nothing and covers remote employees who move).

Variations that matter in drafting:
- *New Jersey* protects own-time inventions made without the employer's equipment, supplies, facilities, *or information* (not only trade secrets) — broader than California.
- *Minnesota* and *Washington* (and the Kansas notice) carve back inventions that relate "*directly*" to the employer's business.
- *Minnesota's* subdivision 1 is drafted in the negative with "or," which read literally protects an own-time invention that fails *either* test; quote it exactly and do not paraphrase.
- *Utah* lets an employer require assignment of any "employment invention," a term that includes inventions "related to the industry or trade of the employer" and those made "on the employer's time" — the most employer-favorable statute.
- *Kansas* (§ 44-130(d)) and *Washington* (RCW 49.44.150) require the employee to disclose all inventions being developed, even excluded ones; *North Carolina* (§ 66-57.2(a)) and *California* (§ 2871) permit the employer to require confidential disclosure. Section 4.4 requires confidential disclosure of all inventions for review.

**Default for the exclusion.** Section 4.3 offers two alternatives. Alternative A excludes only what the employee's governing statute requires. Alternative B (the default) applies the California § 2870 standard to *every* employee as a matter of contract, plus any more protective statute. Alternative B simplifies administration for distributed teams and reduces enforceability fights; Alternative A captures more for the Company in Utah, Nevada, and states without a statute. Use Alternative A only with a reason.

**DTSA notice.** The employer "shall provide notice of the immunity … in any contract or agreement with an employee that governs the use of a trade secret or other confidential information" (18 U.S.C. § 1833(b)(3)(A)). Without it, the employer may not recover exemplary damages or attorney fees under the DTSA against that employee (§ 1833(b)(3)(C)). The notice may instead cross-reference a policy document that sets out the employer's reporting policy (§ 1833(b)(3)(B)), but the full notice in Section 3.3 is safer. The requirement applies to agreements "entered into or updated" after May 11, 2016.

**Protected activity.** Section 3 preserves the employee's right to communicate with government agencies without notice to the Company (SEC Rule 21F-17(a) prohibits "any person" from impeding communications with the SEC, "including enforcing, or threatening to enforce, a confidentiality agreement"), to receive whistleblower awards, to testify, to discuss wages and working conditions (NLRA § 7, 29 U.S.C. § 157, for non-supervisory employees; Cal. Lab. Code § 232), and to disclose sexual assault or harassment (Speak Out Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 19401–19404, which makes pre-dispute nondisclosure and nondisparagement clauses unenforceable as to those disputes). California requires specific language in any confidentiality provision of an agreement related to employment (Cal. Gov. Code § 12964.5), included in Section 3.2.

> **Verify:** As of November 2, 2026, Stericycle, 372 NLRB No. 113 (2023) (work rules), and McLaren Macomb, 372 NLRB No. 58 (2023) (severance agreements), remain binding Board law; the Board applied McLaren as "extant precedent" in Prime Communications, LP, 374 NLRB No. 88 (Apr. 7, 2026) while signaling openness to reconsidering it, and the General Counsel has asked the Board to overrule both (GC Memo 26-04, Aug. 26, 2026, naming Valley Radiology (McLaren) and Honeywell (Stericycle) as vehicles). GC 25-05 (Feb. 14, 2025) rescinded the prior General Counsel's guidance memos. Check for an overruling decision before relying on narrower drafting. The form's carve-outs satisfy the 2023 standards, which is the conservative course, and NLRA protection does not extend to supervisors and managers (29 U.S.C. § 152(3), (11)).

**Restrictive covenants — default posture.** This form contains **no non-compete**. Non-competes for employees are void in California (Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 16600, 16600.5), Minnesota (Minn. Stat. § 181.988, for agreements entered into on or after July 1, 2023), North Dakota (N.D. Cent. Code § 9-08-06), and Oklahoma (15 O.S. § 219A), are limited by income thresholds, notice periods, and garden-leave requirements in a growing list of states, and, as a practical matter, are rarely worth their enforcement cost for an early-stage company. Trade-secret law plus a good confidentiality clause protects what matters. The Federal Trade Commission's 2024 nationwide non-compete rule never took effect: it was set aside in Ryan LLC v. FTC (N.D. Tex. Aug. 20, 2024), the FTC voted on September 5, 2025 to dismiss its appeals and accede to vacatur, and it removed the rule (16 C.F.R. part 910) from the Code of Federal Regulations effective February 12, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 6507). The FTC continues to attack individual non-competes case-by-case under Section 5 of the FTC Act (consent orders against Gateway Services (2025) and Rollins (2026), plus warning letters). Washington will join the ban states on June 30, 2027, when ESHB 1155 (ch. 149, Laws of 2026) voids all non-competes regardless of when signed; Tennessee bars non-competes for employees earning under $70,000 for agreements governed by its 2026 law (effective July 1, 2026). If a non-compete is commercially essential (for example, a senior executive in a state that enforces them, or a sale-of-business setting), draft it as a separate, state-specific agreement with counsel in that state.

Employee and customer **non-solicitation** covenants are offered as options in Sections 7.2 and 7.3:

> **Verify:** Income thresholds and state restrictive-covenant statutes change annually and new states act every legislative session. Before including Section 7.2 or 7.3, check references/employment-and-ip.md and the employee's state statute for the current threshold, notice period, and any 2026 amendments.

**Consideration.** A CIIAA signed at hire is supported by the offer of employment. For a current employee asked to sign mid-employment, continued at-will employment is sufficient consideration in most states for confidentiality and invention-assignment terms, but restrictive covenants often need more: Illinois requires "adequate consideration" (at least two years of continued employment after signing, or other professional or financial benefits, 820 ILCS 90/5); Washington requires independent consideration for post-hire non-competes; Pennsylvania requires new consideration for post-hire restrictive covenants; and several courts have held continued employment insufficient. For mid-employment signers, provide a modest cash payment, an equity grant, or a raise, and recite it in Section 10.1. Utah expressly treats employment as sufficient consideration for assignment of employment inventions (Utah Code § 34-39-3(5)).

**Choice of law and forum.** Default to the law of the state where the employee primarily resides and works (Section 10.2). A California employee who primarily resides and works in California may void a clause requiring adjudication outside California or depriving the employee of California law, unless the employee was individually represented by counsel in negotiating the choice-of-law or forum term (Cal. Lab. Code § 925(a), (e)); and California's non-compete statutes apply "regardless of where and when the contract was signed" (Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600.5(a)). Colorado, Washington, Minnesota, Massachusetts, and Illinois have similar anti-evasion rules for restrictive covenants. A Delaware choice of law for a non-Delaware employee mostly invites a fight.

**Common mistakes the form avoids.**
1. "Agree to assign" without "hereby assign."
2. Missing state exclusion notices (California, Illinois, Kansas, Minnesota, Washington require written notice).
3. Missing DTSA notice.
4. Over-broad confidentiality that sweeps in wages and working conditions (NLRA and California risk) or forbids reporting to regulators (SEC risk).
5. "Trailer" clauses assigning inventions conceived *after* employment ends. The Federal Circuit held a California post-employment assignment clause void under § 16600 where it was not limited to inventions using the employer's trade secrets (Whitewater West Indus., Ltd. v. Alleshouse, 981 F.3d 1045 (Fed. Cir. 2020)). Section 4.9 is an optional, narrow presumption tied to Confidential Information.
6. Prior inventions listed vaguely or not at all; Section 4.1 provides a license if the employee incorporates them, and Exhibit A forces a disclosure.
7. Signing after the employee has already created key IP without covering the earlier period (Section 1.9).
8. Using "work made for hire" language in contractor agreements in California (not relevant for employees, but see consulting-agreement).

**Deadlines triggered by signing.** None by statute, but: deliver Exhibit B at signing (the statutory notices must be given "at the time the agreement is made"); Illinois requires that an employee be given at least 14 calendar days to review a covenant not to solicit (820 ILCS 90/20); Colorado requires separate written notice of a non-solicit before acceptance of the offer (for new hires) or at least 14 days before the effective date (for current workers) (Colo. Rev. Stat. § 8-2-113(4)); and record a short-form assignment with the USPTO within three months after any patent assignment that must be recorded (35 U.S.C. § 261).

# EMPLOYEE CONFIDENTIAL INFORMATION AND INVENTION ASSIGNMENT AGREEMENT

This Employee Confidential Information and Invention Assignment Agreement (this "**Agreement**") is entered into as of {{effective_date}} (the "**Effective Date**") by and between {{company_name}}, a Delaware corporation (together with its successors and assigns, the "**Company**"), and {{employee_name}} ("**Employee**").

In consideration of Employee's employment or continued employment by the Company, the compensation and benefits paid to Employee, Employee's access to Confidential Information, and the mutual covenants in this Agreement, the receipt and sufficiency of which are acknowledged, the Company and Employee agree as follows:

## 1. Definitions

**1.1 "Company Group"** means the Company and each entity that, now or in the future, directly or indirectly controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with the Company. References in Articles 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 to the Company include each member of the Company Group for which Employee performs services or about which Employee receives Confidential Information.

**1.2 "Company Inventions"** means all Inventions that Employee, alone or jointly with others, conceives, develops, creates, reduces to practice, or otherwise makes during the Relationship Period, in each case to the extent the Invention (a) results from or is suggested by any work Employee performs for the Company Group; (b) relates, at the time of conception, development, or reduction to practice, to the business of the Company Group or to its actual or demonstrably anticipated research or development; or (c) is conceived, developed, or made using any equipment, supplies, facilities, Confidential Information, or other resources of the Company Group, or during Employee's working time for the Company. Company Inventions do not include Nonassignable Inventions or Prior Inventions.

**1.3 "Confidential Information"** means all information, in any form or medium and whether or not marked as confidential, that is not generally known to the public and that relates to the actual or anticipated business, products, services, research, or development of the Company Group or to its customers, suppliers, partners, investors, or other business relations, that Employee learns, receives, or creates during the Relationship Period, including: (a) technical information such as Inventions, source and object code, algorithms, models, model weights, prompts, training and evaluation data, data sets, software architecture, designs, specifications, prototypes, formulas, processes, techniques, research, test results, and know-how; (b) product plans, roadmaps, and unreleased features; (c) business information such as business plans and strategies, financial information, forecasts, budgets, pricing, costs, margins, sales and marketing plans, and information about the Company Group's capitalization, financings, and investors; (d) the identities, preferences, contract terms, and contact persons of customers, prospective customers, suppliers, and partners; (e) non-public personal information of employees, contractors, customers, and users, including compensation of other individuals obtained through Employee's job duties (such as from personnel or payroll records) rather than from those individuals; (f) security practices, credentials, and system configurations; and (g) Third Party Information. Confidential Information does not include information that is or becomes generally available to the public other than through a breach by Employee of this Agreement or of a duty owed to the Company Group, or any information described in Section 3.1.

**1.4 "Intellectual Property Rights"** means all rights, anywhere in the world, in and to patents, patent applications, utility models, and statutory invention registrations (including provisionals, continuations, continuations-in-part, divisionals, reissues, reexaminations, and extensions); copyrights, works of authorship, and mask works, whether or not registered, together with all registrations, applications, renewals, and extensions; trade secrets and know-how; trademarks, service marks, trade names, trade dress, logos, and domain names, together with the associated goodwill; moral rights and rights of attribution and integrity; rights in designs, databases, and data; and all other intellectual or industrial property rights, together with all rights to sue for past, present, and future infringement or misappropriation of any of them.

**1.5 "Inventions"** means inventions (whether or not patentable), discoveries, improvements, ideas, concepts, designs, developments, processes, methods, formulas, algorithms, models, data, compilations, software, documentation, works of authorship, mask works, trademarks, trade secrets, know-how, and all other subject matter protectable by any Intellectual Property Right, together with all Intellectual Property Rights in them.

**1.6 "Nonassignable Inventions"** has the meaning given in Section 4.3.

**1.7 "Prior Inventions"** means all Inventions that Employee, alone or jointly with others, conceived, developed, created, reduced to practice, or acquired before the Relationship Period begins, that Employee owns (in whole or in part) or has an interest in, and that relate to the business or the actual or demonstrably anticipated research or development of the Company Group.

**1.8 "Protected Activity"** has the meaning given in Section 3.1.

**1.9 "Relationship Period"** means the entire period during which Employee performs services for the Company Group in any capacity (including as an employee, intern, consultant, advisor, founder, or director), beginning on {{relationship_start_date}} (even if that date is before the Effective Date) and ending on the Termination Date.

**1.10 "Termination Date"** means the date on which Employee's employment with the Company Group ends for any reason, or, if Employee continues to provide services to the Company Group in another capacity, the date on which all such services end.

**1.11 "Third Party Information"** means confidential or proprietary information that the Company Group has received from any third party (including customers, suppliers, partners, and prospective acquirers or investors) subject to a duty to maintain its confidentiality or to use it only for limited purposes.

## 2. Confidential Information

**2.1 Nonuse and Nondisclosure.** During the Relationship Period and at all times thereafter, Employee shall hold all Confidential Information in strict confidence and shall not (a) use any Confidential Information except as required to perform Employee's duties for the Company Group, or (b) disclose any Confidential Information to any person other than employees, contractors, and advisors of the Company Group who need to know it for the Company's business and are bound by confidentiality obligations, except (i) with the prior written authorization of an officer of the Company, (ii) as permitted by Article 3, or (iii) as required by law in accordance with Section 2.4. Employee's obligations with respect to Confidential Information that is not a trade secret continue for so long as the information remains confidential, and with respect to trade secrets, for so long as they remain trade secrets under applicable law.

**2.2 Security; Third-Party Tools.** Employee shall comply with all Company policies concerning the security, classification, handling, and storage of Confidential Information, including policies on passwords, multi-factor authentication, encryption, removable media, and personal devices. Employee shall not upload, paste, or otherwise input Confidential Information into any generative artificial-intelligence tool, code assistant, translation service, file-sharing service, personal email account, or other third-party application or service, except a tool approved by the Company for that use and in accordance with Company policy.

**2.3 Third Party Information.** Employee shall hold all Third Party Information in strict confidence and shall use and disclose it only as necessary to perform Employee's duties for the Company Group and in accordance with the Company Group's agreement with the third party.

**2.4 Compelled Disclosure.** If Employee is required by subpoena, court order, or other legal process to disclose Confidential Information, Employee shall, to the extent legally permitted and except in connection with Protected Activity, give the Company prompt written notice so that the Company may seek a protective order or other remedy, and shall disclose only the Confidential Information that is legally required to be disclosed.

**2.5 Former Employers' Information.** Employee shall not, during the Relationship Period, improperly use or disclose any confidential or proprietary information or trade secret of any former or current employer or other person to whom Employee owes a duty of confidentiality, and shall not bring onto the premises or systems of the Company Group any unpublished document, file, code, or other property belonging to any such person unless that person has consented in writing. Employee shall use in the performance of Employee's duties only information that is generally known and used by persons with training and experience comparable to Employee's, that is common knowledge in the industry, that is otherwise legally in the public domain, or that is provided or developed by the Company Group.

## 3. Protected Activity; Required Notices

**3.1 Protected Activity Not Prohibited.** Nothing in this Agreement or in any other agreement with or policy of the Company Group prohibits, restricts, or requires notice to or approval from the Company Group for, any of the following (collectively, "**Protected Activity**"):

(a) filing a charge or complaint with, reporting a possible violation of law (including criminal conduct) to, communicating with, providing documents or other information to, or participating or testifying in any investigation or proceeding conducted by any federal, state, or local governmental agency, commission, or regulatory or law enforcement authority, including the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the National Labor Relations Board, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, the Department of Labor, the Department of Justice, any state attorney general, any state or local human rights or fair employment agency (such as the New York State Division of Human Rights or a local commission on human rights), and any state or local labor agency;

(b) receiving an award from any governmental agency for information provided to it;

(c) testifying truthfully in any legal or administrative proceeding, or responding truthfully to a valid subpoena;

(d) discussing or disclosing Employee's own wages, hours, or other terms and conditions of employment, or those of other employees who have shared that information with Employee or that Employee otherwise learned other than through access to Company records in the course of Employee's job duties, or otherwise engaging in concerted activity for mutual aid or protection under Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act;
