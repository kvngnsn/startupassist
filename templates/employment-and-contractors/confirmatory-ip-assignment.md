## About this template

**What it does.** Investors and acquirers test whether the Company owns the code, inventions, designs, data, and brand it is selling. Gaps appear when someone contributed before the Company existed, contributed under an oral understanding, signed a contractor agreement without an IP clause, signed a clause that only promised to assign in the future, or left before signing anything. This agreement closes those gaps after the fact. It (1) confirms that the Company owns the contributions, (2) presently assigns any rights that remain with the contributor, with an effective date reaching back to creation ("nunc pro tunc"), (3) provides a license and covenant not to sue as a fallback for any rights that cannot be assigned, (4) obtains further-assurance cooperation and a power of attorney for recordings, and (5) usually takes a release of claims to compensation for the contributions.

**Why a present assignment is needed even if the Company "already owns it."**
- *Copyright.* Only an *employee* acting within the scope of employment creates a work made for hire automatically. A contractor's work is a work made for hire only if it falls within one of the nine commissioned-work categories in 17 U.S.C. § 101 *and* the parties signed a written instrument saying so. Software and most startup deliverables do not fit those categories. Otherwise the author owns the copyright until a signed written transfer (17 U.S.C. § 204(a)).
- *Patents.* Inventors own their inventions until they assign them. An agreement that says the contributor "agrees to assign" or "will assign" is only a promise to assign in the future; it does not transfer legal title, and a later "do hereby assign" to someone else can take priority (Stanford v. Roche, 583 F.3d 832, 841–42 (Fed. Cir. 2009), aff'd, 563 U.S. 776 (2011)). Each joint owner of a patent may make, use, sell, and license the invention without the other owners' consent and without accounting (35 U.S.C. § 262), so an unassigned co-inventor can license a competitor, and all co-owners generally must join as plaintiffs to sue for infringement.
- *Trademarks and domains.* A trademark must be assigned together with the goodwill of the business it symbolizes; an intent-to-use application generally cannot be assigned before an allegation of use is filed except to a successor to the business (15 U.S.C. § 1060(a)(1)). Domain names and social-media handles sit in personal accounts and need an account transfer, not just a paper assignment.

**What "nunc pro tunc" does and does not do.** Stating that an assignment is effective as of the date of creation helps document that the parties always intended the Company to own the work and aligns the record with the Company's financial statements and prior representations. It does *not*: (a) give the Company standing to sue for infringement in a lawsuit filed before the assignment was signed (Enzo APA & Son, Inc. v. Geapag A.G., 134 F.3d 1090, 1093–94 (Fed. Cir. 1998)); (b) defeat rights a third party acquired in the interim (a prior assignee, a university or employer with a present-assignment clause, or a licensee); or (c) change the tax year in which consideration is taxed. Sign the confirmatory assignment *before* any enforcement action or financing closing.

**Assignment or quitclaim.** Use **Alternative A** (assignment with title representations) when the contributor clearly authored or invented the material and will stand behind it. Use **Alternative B** (quitclaim) when the contributor's role is uncertain, the contributor will not give representations, or the purpose is simply to eliminate any possible claim (for example, a person who sat in on early brainstorming). A quitclaim conveys whatever interest the contributor has, if any, without warranting that there is one.

**Consideration.** A completed present assignment of property is effective without consideration, but the release, covenants, and further-assurance obligations need it, and recited, actually paid consideration forecloses later arguments about failure of consideration or an unenforceable gift. Choices:
- *Cash* (common: a modest fixed payment). Report on Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC as applicable; a payment to a current or former *employee* for work done as an employee is wages subject to withholding.
- *Shares or options.* Needs Board approval (8 Del. C. § 152 permits intangible property as consideration), a securities-law exemption (Rule 701 for compensatory grants to natural persons who provided bona fide services, or Section 4(a)(2) / Regulation D), and a fair-market-value analysis; the contributor recognizes income under I.R.C. § 83 unless the transfer qualifies under § 351 at formation.
- *Nominal consideration plus mutual covenants* (e.g., $10 or $100 and the Company's release of claims against the contributor) for friendly contributors and quitclaims.

**Third parties.** If the contributor was employed or enrolled elsewhere when contributing, that employer or university may already own the work under its own invention assignment or IP policy. Check the contributor's agreements. California (Lab. Code § 2870) and several other states protect inventions made on the employee's own time without employer resources that do not relate to the employer's business, which can help, but a written release or license from the third party is the only reliable cure.

**Community property.** In community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), a married contributor's spouse may have a community interest in intellectual property created during marriage (see In re Marriage of Worth, 195 Cal. App. 3d 768 (1987) (copyrights)). The optional Spousal Consent removes the question.

**Release.** Section 8 (optional) releases claims to ownership, royalties, and compensation for the contributions. For a California assignor, include the Civil Code § 1542 waiver. Do not use this release for unpaid wages of a current or former employee (wage claims are generally non-waivable without a bona fide dispute; see Cal. Lab. Code § 206.5) and do not use it to release age-discrimination claims (OWBPA rules apply; use separation-agreement-and-release).

**DTSA notice.** If the Assignor is an individual who performed work as a contractor or consultant, the confidentiality covenant in Section 7 "governs the use of a trade secret," so include the 18 U.S.C. § 1833(b) notice (Section 7.3) to preserve exemplary damages and attorney fees.

**Recording.** Record patent assignments with the USPTO within three months after execution to protect against later purchasers and mortgagees without notice (35 U.S.C. § 261). Record copyright transfers with the U.S. Copyright Office; a recorded transfer of a registered work gives constructive notice, and the first-executed transfer prevails if recorded within one month after execution (two months if executed abroad) or before a later transfer is recorded (17 U.S.C. § 205(c)–(d)). Exhibit C is a short-form instrument suitable for recording so the full agreement (with consideration and release) stays confidential.

**Common mistakes the form avoids.** "Agree to assign" language; no fallback license for unassignable rights; omitting moral-rights waivers for foreign contributors; ignoring trademarks, domains, and accounts; failing to schedule the specific repositories, patents, and registrations; and assuming nunc pro tunc language cures an existing standing defect.

**Deadlines triggered by signing.** Patent recording within three months (35 U.S.C. § 261); copyright recordation within one month for priority (17 U.S.C. § 205(d)); § 83(b) election within 30 days if unvested shares are issued as consideration; California § 25102(o) or other state notice if shares are issued under the plan.

## 1. Definitions

**1.1 "Assigned IP"** means (a) the Contributions; (b) all Intellectual Property Rights in the Contributions; (c) all rights to claim priority to, and to file applications for registration of, any of the foregoing anywhere in the world; and (d) all claims and causes of action for past, present, and future infringement, misappropriation, or other violation of any of the foregoing, and all rights to damages, profits, and other recoveries from them.

**1.2 "Company Business"** means the business of the Company [[Optional: and the Predecessor]] as conducted or demonstrably planned at any time during the Contribution Period, including {{company_business_description}}.

**1.3 "Contributions"** means all inventions (whether or not patentable), discoveries, improvements, ideas, designs, processes, methods, formulas, algorithms, models, data, software (in source and object code form), documentation, works of authorship, graphics, user interfaces, names, logos, marks, domain names, and other materials, that the Assignor, alone or jointly with others, conceived, created, developed, reduced to practice, or contributed to during the Contribution Period (a) for or on behalf of the Company [[Optional: or the Predecessor]], (b) relating to the Company Business, or (c) through use of any confidential information, equipment, or resources of the Company [[Optional: or the Predecessor]], including those described in Exhibit A, but excluding the Excluded Materials.

**1.4 "Excluded Materials"** means the inventions, works, and materials listed in Exhibit B.

**1.5 "Intellectual Property Rights"** means all rights, anywhere in the world, in and to patents, patent applications, utility models, and statutory invention registrations (including provisionals, continuations, continuations-in-part, divisionals, reissues, reexaminations, and extensions); copyrights, works of authorship, and mask works, whether or not registered, and all registrations and applications for them; trade secrets and know-how; trademarks, service marks, trade names, trade dress, logos, and domain names and social-media identifiers, together with the goodwill of the business connected with their use and symbolized by them; moral rights and rights of attribution and integrity; rights in designs and databases; and all other intellectual or industrial property rights.
