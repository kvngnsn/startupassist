## About this template

**What it does.** This is the board's organizational action. In one document the directors ratify the incorporator's acts, confirm the bylaws, elect officers, set the fiscal year and principal office, make shares uncertificated, authorize bank accounts and tax registrations, approve the standard formation agreements, determine the fair market value of the Common Stock and the value of any technology contributed, and authorize the founder stock issuances. Under § 108(c) and § 141(f) the board may act by unanimous written consent instead of meeting.

**Sequencing (state it in the document).**
1. Certificate of incorporation filed (Division's filing date and time).
2. Action of sole incorporator effective (adopts bylaws, fixes board size, elects directors, incorporator resigns).
3. **This consent effective** — after Step 2. If on the same day, state the time (this form has an effective-time line).
4. Founder RSPAs, technology assignment agreements, and CIIAAs signed and consideration delivered — on or after this consent's effective time. The RSPA closing date is the issuance date and starts the 30-day 83(b) clock.
5. § 151(f) notices delivered; stock ledger entries made.
6. Founders file 83(b) elections within 30 days; deliver copies to the Company.
7. California § 25102(f) notice (if any founder purchased in California) within 15 calendar days after the first sale.

**Definite effective date and time.** The form states "Effective as of [time] on [date]." Never use "effective when signed by all directors" without a date: under § 141(f) the action is taken only when every director has consented, so an undated consent leaves the effective time — and the validity of every act taken in reliance on it — uncertain. If a director signs early, § 141(f) lets the director's consent take effect at a future time not more than 60 days later, provided the person is a director then.

**No backdating; no "fix-up" stockholder consent.** If the company has already been operating without this consent (for example, founders were issued shares before a board existed, or the board acted without bylaws), this form is the wrong tool. Do not date it in the past. Unauthorized stock issuances are void under Delaware law (*STAAR Surgical Co. v. Waggoner*, 588 A.2d 1130 (Del. 1991)), and the statutory cure is ratification under § 204 (with a certificate of validation if needed) or court validation under § 205. Likewise, do not have the founders sign a stockholder consent "electing" the directors who have been acting all along: directors elected by the incorporator are validly in office until their successors are elected (§ 141(b)), and a retroactive stockholder election creates the appearance of a defect it does not cure.

**Founder stock — the core decisions.**
- *Price.* At formation, before the company has assets or operations, the fair market value of Common Stock is nominal. The board determines it (commonly at par, $0.00001) and every founder buying in the same round pays the same price. A founder paying less than another for identical stock at the same time receives a bargain element that is compensation income.
- *Consideration.* Cash, technology, or both (§ 152 permits "cash, any tangible or intangible property or any benefit to the corporation"). The board must determine the value of non-cash consideration; its judgment is conclusive absent actual fraud (§ 152(d)); value must be at least aggregate par (§ 153(a)). Do not issue stock "for services": that makes the founder's receipt of stock compensation income under § 83 at its full value and, if unvested, taxable as it vests unless an 83(b) election is filed.
- *Vesting and repurchase.* Approved through the form RSPA; the schedule for each founder is in Exhibit B.
- *Interested directors.* Each founder-director is interested in his or her own issuance (and arguably in the others'). The 2025 § 144(a)(1) safe harbor requires approval by a majority of disinterested directors — or, if a majority are not disinterested, by a committee of at least two disinterested directors — or by disinterested stockholders. At formation there are usually neither. The issuances are therefore supported by § 144(a)(3) fairness: uniform price equal to fair market value, identical terms for all founders except agreed share counts and vesting, full disclosure. Section 14 recites those facts. If one founder receives materially different terms, consider having the other directors approve that founder's terms separately after disclosure.
- *Share reserve arithmetic.* Founder shares plus any plan reserve must not exceed authorized shares. Example: 10,000,000 authorized; founders 4,000,000 + 4,000,000; plan reserve 1,500,000; unissued and unreserved 500,000.

**Securities law.** Founder sales rely on Securities Act § 4(a)(2) (private offering). No Form D is required unless the company relies on Rule 506. State law: in **California**, the DFPI's position is that a newly incorporated company issuing shares to founders in California must file a Limited Offering Exemption Notice under Corporations Code § 25102(f) within 15 calendar days after the first sale in California (10 Cal. Code Regs. § 260.102.14(b)), electronically through the DFPI's online system (FRANSES, which replaced the older self-service portal), with the § 25608(c) fee (for a value of $25,000 or less, $25) and, for a non-California issuer, a consent to service of process. Founders who are officers, directors or promoters do not count toward the 35-purchaser limit, but the notice is still filed. Failure to file does not destroy the exemption, but late filing triggers a fee equal to the fee for a qualification under § 25110. Other states: check the founder's state of residence.

> **Verify:** Confirm the current § 25608(c) fee schedule (as of mid-2025: $25 for $25,000 or less; $35 for $25,001–$100,000; $50 for $100,001–$500,000; $150 for $500,001–$1,000,000; $300 above $1,000,000) and the current DFPI electronic filing URL and consent-to-service form before filing; the statute and rule text were confirmed through a mirror of the official code because the official site blocks automated access.

**Beneficial ownership reporting.** As of August 2026, FinCEN's final rule removes all entities created in the United States from the definition of "reporting company" under the Corporate Transparency Act; a newly formed Delaware corporation files no beneficial ownership information (BOI) report. Foreign entities registered to do business in the United States still report. Banks still collect beneficial-ownership information when opening accounts under the customer due diligence rule (31 C.F.R. § 1010.230). Section 22 records this.

> **Verify:** Confirm, at the time of formation, that FinCEN's August 2026 final rule remains in effect without further amendment and that no state has enacted its own beneficial ownership filing requirement applicable to the company (for example, New York's LLC Transparency Act applies to LLCs, not corporations).

**Equity incentive plan.** Optional Section 19 adopts a plan by cross-reference to the equity-compensation family. Never leave the share reserve blank: an option granted against a blank or insufficient reserve is a defective issuance. Incentive stock options require stockholder approval within 12 months before or after board adoption (I.R.C. § 422(b)(1)). Options must be priced at fair market value, which after the first months of operations generally means an independent § 409A valuation.

**Fiscal year and tax elections.** The first federal return (Form 1120) establishes the tax year. Section 11 also notes the § 248 election to deduct up to $5,000 of organizational expenditures (reduced dollar for dollar above $50,000) and amortize the rest over 180 months, which is deemed made unless the company elects otherwise (Treas. Reg. § 1.248-1(c)).
