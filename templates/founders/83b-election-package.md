## About this template

**What the election does.** Under § 83(a), property received in connection with services and subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture (such as founder stock the Company can repurchase at cost if the founder leaves) is taxed when it vests: the founder recognizes ordinary income equal to the value at vesting minus the amount paid. A § 83(b) election instead taxes the property at transfer, on its value then (ignoring lapse restrictions) minus the amount paid. For founders who pay full fair market value at formation, that income is **zero**, and the election (1) eliminates ordinary income at each vesting date, (2) starts the capital-gain holding period at the transfer date, and (3) starts the § 1202 qualified small business stock holding period at the transfer date (Treas. Reg. § 1.83-4(a); without the election, the holding period starts only on vesting, § 83(f)). The downside is limited: if the stock is later forfeited, the deductible loss is limited to the amount paid minus any amount realized (Treas. Reg. § 1.83-2(a)), which at par value is negligible.

**The deadline is absolute.** The election must be filed **not later than 30 days after the date the property is transferred** (§ 83(b)(2); Treas. Reg. § 1.83-2(b)). It may also be filed before the transfer. There is no extension and no late-election relief in ordinary circumstances. If the 30th day is a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, filing on the next business day is timely (§ 7503; Form 15620 says the same). Count from the closing date of the stock purchase agreement: for a transfer on January 6, 2027, the deadline is February 5, 2027.

**How to file (as of November 2026).** Three acceptable routes:
1. **Form 15620 by mail.** The IRS released Form 15620 in November 2024; the current revision is **April 2025**. Use is voluntary.
2. **A written statement by mail** meeting Treas. Reg. § 1.83-2(e) (Part C of this package). The IRS confirms either form is acceptable.
3. **Online submission of Form 15620.** Since mid-2025 the IRS "mobile-friendly forms" page has offered Form 15620 for completion with an IRS Online Account, with the option to submit online or download for mailing. The IRS has not issued a formal announcement; law-firm reports describe early field limits on the online form (share-count caps and decimal places for per-share values — initially two decimal places, later reportedly four). **A $0.00001 per-share value has five decimal places, so the online form may not accept it.** For typical founder elections at par, file on paper by certified mail unless you have confirmed the online form accepts the exact figures.

> **Verify:** At the time of filing, check (a) whether the IRS has posted a newer revision of Form 15620; (b) the current status and field limits of online submission at irs.gov/forms-pubs/mobile-friendly-forms; and (c) the current mailing address on the IRS "Where to File" page for Form 1040 filers not enclosing a payment. These items changed in 2024–2026 and may change again.

**Where to mail.** The regulation requires filing "with the internal revenue office with which the person who performed the services files his return" (§ 1.83-2(c)). Form 15620 contains no address table. IRS Publication 525 tells taxpayers to mail the statement to the address listed for their state under "Are requesting a refund or are not enclosing a check or money order" in the Form 1040 instructions' "Where Do You File?" table. Part A, step 6, reproduces the table published in July 2026.

**Proof of timely filing.** Under § 7502 and Treas. Reg. § 301.7502-1, a document mailed by U.S. certified or registered mail is treated as filed on the postmark date, and the postmarked certified-mail receipt is the taxpayer's proof. Other than direct proof of actual delivery, registered mail, certified mail and IRS-designated private delivery services are the exclusive means of proving delivery. Private carriers must use IRS street addresses, not P.O. boxes. Use **USPS Certified Mail with a return receipt**, have the clerk postmark the receipt, and keep both.

**Copies.** The taxpayer must furnish a copy to the person for whom the services are performed (the Company) and, if different, the transferee (§ 1.83-2(d)). Since the 2016 regulation (T.D. 9779), the taxpayer **no longer attaches a copy to the income tax return** for transfers on or after January 1, 2016. Rev. Proc. 2012-29's sample election still contains the old attachment sentence; ignore it.

**Required contents (§ 1.83-2(e)).** The statement must be signed by the person making the election, state that it is made under § 83(b), and contain: (1) name, address and taxpayer identification number; (2) a description of each property; (3) the date or dates of transfer and the taxable year; (4) the nature of the restrictions; (5) fair market value at transfer, determined without regard to lapse restrictions; (6) the amount paid, if any; and (7) a statement that copies have been furnished to the other persons required by § 1.83-2(d). Form 15620 covers each item; box 9 (the service recipient's information) is optional on the form.

**Signature.** Only the taxpayer signs (§ 1.83-2(e)). IRM Exhibit 10.10.1-2 lists § 83(b) elections among documents the IRS will accept with electronic or digital signatures (signed on or after August 28, 2020). For a paper filing, a handwritten signature remains the conservative choice. A spouse's signature is **not** required by the regulation; see Part F for community property practice.

**Revocation.** An election may be revoked only with the Commissioner's consent, generally available only for a mistake of fact as to the underlying transaction, requested within 60 days after the mistake is discovered (§ 1.83-2(f)). A drop in value is not a mistake of fact.

**Technology as consideration.** If the founder paid for the stock by assigning technology, the "amount paid" is the value of the property transferred, as the board determined it (matching the stock purchase agreement and board consent). The election still reports zero income if that value equals the stock's fair market value. Section 351 treatment of the technology transfer is a separate question for the founder's tax return.

**No-SSN founders.** Box 1 requires a taxpayer identification number. A founder without an SSN should apply for an ITIN (Form W-7). Practitioners commonly file the election on time with "Applied for" or the TIN left blank and a note that a TIN application is pending, then supplement when issued; there is no IRS guidance squarely approving this.

> **Verify:** For a founder without an SSN or ITIN, confirm current IRS practice on filing the election before a TIN is assigned, and whether the online route is available to that founder (it requires an IRS Online Account).

**State returns.** Most states that tax income follow the federal treatment of restricted property and do not require a separate state election.

> **Verify:** Confirm the treatment in the founder's state of residence (and any state where services are performed); some states have historically required a copy with the state return or have nonconforming rules.

**Company's role.** The Company does not file the election and should say so in writing (the founder stock purchase agreement does). It should (1) deliver this package at signing with the deadline written on the first page; (2) receive and file the founder's copy and proof of mailing; (3) record receipt on the stock ledger; and (4) diarize a check-in at day 20. Diligence for every priced round asks for each founder's filed 83(b) and proof of mailing.

# PART A — INSTRUCTIONS TO THE TAXPAYER

1. **Read and decide.** The election is your decision and your responsibility, not the Company's. The Company and its counsel do not file it for you and do not give you tax advice. Consult your own tax advisor. For founders who paid full fair market value for their shares, the election typically results in no current tax and is almost always advisable; if you do not file it, you may owe ordinary income tax as your shares vest, on their value at that time.

2. **Choose the form.** Use either (a) **IRS Form 15620** (Rev. April 2025), completed as described in Part B, or (b) the **written statement** in Part C. Do not use both. Do not alter the substance of the written statement except to fill in the blanks.

3. **Complete it accurately.** Use the figures in the Drafting inputs, which match your stock purchase agreement. The description of restrictions must match your vesting schedule.

4. **Sign and date it.** Sign by hand in ink. Keep a scanned copy of the signed document before mailing.

5. **Make copies.** Make three copies of the signed election: one for your records, one for the Company (Part E), and one to serve as a "return copy" if you wish the IRS to stamp and return it (optional; include a self-addressed stamped envelope and the cover letter in Part D asks for it).

6. **Mail to the IRS.** Mail the signed original to the IRS office where you file your federal income tax return. Under IRS Publication 525, use the address listed for your state in the Form 1040 "Where Do You File?" table for taxpayers **not enclosing a payment**. As published by the IRS in July 2026 (confirm before mailing):

|---|---|
| Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, New Mexico, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas | Department of the Treasury, Internal Revenue Service, Austin, TX 73301-0002 |
| Connecticut, Delaware, District of Columbia, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin | Department of the Treasury, Internal Revenue Service, Kansas City, MO 64999-0002 |

> **Verify:** Re-check this table on the IRS Form 1040 "Where to File" page on the day of mailing. The IRS revises it, and the general "Where to File Paper Tax Returns" page has lagged the Form 1040 page in the past.

7. **Use certified mail.** At the post office, send the envelope by **USPS Certified Mail, Return Receipt Requested**, and ask the clerk to postmark the certified-mail receipt. Keep the postmarked receipt and, when it arrives, the return receipt (green card or electronic). These are your proof of timely filing. If you use a private delivery service, use only an IRS-designated service and the IRS street address for that service center (P.O. boxes cannot receive private deliveries).

8. **Or submit online (only if it works for your numbers).** If you have an IRS Online Account, you may complete and submit Form 15620 through the IRS mobile-friendly forms page. Confirm the form accepts your exact share count and per-share values (values such as $0.00001 have more decimal places than the online form has reportedly allowed). Save the submission confirmation as a PDF. If in doubt, mail.

9. **Give the Company a copy.** Within the 30-day period, deliver to the Company a copy of the signed election together with a copy of your postmarked certified-mail receipt (or online confirmation), using the transmittal in Part E.

10. **Do not attach it to your tax return.** For transfers on or after January 1, 2016, you are no longer required to attach a copy of the election to your income tax return. Keep your copy and proof of mailing permanently, with your records for the shares.

11. **If you are married.** See Part F.

12. **If anything goes wrong** (you missed the deadline, the IRS returns the envelope, or you discover an error in the figures), contact a tax advisor immediately. An election cannot be revoked or made late except in very limited circumstances.

# PART F — NOTE FOR MARRIED TAXPAYERS AND DOMESTIC PARTNERS

1. **Federal law does not require your spouse to sign the election.** Treasury Regulations Section 1.83-2(e) requires only the signature of the person making the election, and Form 15620 has a single signature line.

2. **Community property.** If you live in a community property state (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington or Wisconsin) or have opted into community property treatment, your spouse or registered domestic partner may have a community interest in the shares. Your spouse should sign the Spousal Consent attached to your stock purchase agreement, which binds the community interest to the vesting, repurchase and transfer provisions. Some practitioners also have the spouse sign the election itself as a precaution; doing so is not required and does not hurt.

3. **Joint returns.** Filing a joint income tax return does not change who makes the election: the person who performed the services makes it. If you file separately, mail the election to the IRS office where you (the service provider) file.

4. **If you marry, divorce, or move** after the election, no new election is required. Keep your copy and proof of mailing permanently.
