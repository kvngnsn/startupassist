## About this template

**What it does.** A Delaware corporation that operates elsewhere must register ("qualify") as a foreign corporation in each state where it transacts intrastate business, keep a registered agent there, file that state's periodic reports, and register with its tax and employment agencies. This guide explains the test, the consequences of skipping it, and the filings and fees in the states where startups most often operate, then provides a decision worksheet, a tracking table, and a cover letter.

**The "transacting business" test.** Each state defines when a foreign corporation must qualify. Most follow the Model Business Corporation Act approach: "transacting business" is not defined positively, but a list of activities does not by itself count, including maintaining or defending lawsuits, holding board or stockholder meetings, maintaining bank accounts, selling through independent contractors, soliciting orders accepted outside the state, creating or collecting debts, and conducting an isolated transaction completed within a short period. California's version, Cal. Corp. Code § 191, defines transacting intrastate business as "entering into repeated and successive transactions of its business in this state, other than interstate or foreign commerce," with a similar safe-harbor list in § 191(c). Practical rules for startups:
- **An office, or founders or employees working in the state, means qualify.** A company whose founders work from their California homes is transacting intrastate business in California from day one.
- **A first remote employee in a new state almost always means qualify** and register for payroll withholding and unemployment insurance in that state.
- **Customers alone usually do not trigger qualification** (interstate commerce), but they can trigger income or franchise tax nexus and sales tax collection duties, which are separate tests (below).
- **When in doubt, qualify.** Filing costs are modest; the penalties and the inability to sue are not.

**Qualification versus tax nexus.** Corporate qualification and tax "doing business" are different tests. California, for example, treats a corporation as "doing business" for franchise tax purposes if it actively engages in any transaction for profit in California, or if its California sales, property, or payroll exceed bright-line thresholds (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code § 23101; 2025 thresholds: sales $757,070, property $75,707, payroll $75,707, or 25% of the corporation's total). A company can owe California's $800 minimum tax without being qualified. New York's Article 9-A economic nexus threshold is $1,283,000 of New York receipts for tax years beginning 2024 through 2026. Washington's B&O tax applies above $100,000 of Washington gross receipts (RCW 82.04.067). Sales tax collection obligations after South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc., 585 U.S. 162 (2018), are separate again.

> **Verify:** California's 2026 § 23101 thresholds had not been published on the FTB's page as of November 2, 2026 (it showed 2024 and 2025). Check https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/doing-business-in-california.html for the current year's figures.

**Consequences of not qualifying (typical).**
- The corporation cannot maintain a lawsuit in the state's courts on intrastate business until it qualifies and pays back fees, penalties, and taxes (for example, Cal. Corp. Code § 2203(c): qualify and pay a $250 penalty plus fees and back taxes before maintaining an action). Defending suits is still allowed.
- Per-day or lump-sum penalties: California imposes $20 per day of unauthorized intrastate business (§ 2203(a)), assessed for days the court finds willful and set by reference to the corporation's size and willfulness (§ 2203(b)); the current text has no dollar cap. Georgia charges a $500 late-qualification penalty. Illinois assesses back fees and taxes plus penalties (805 ILCS 5/13.70).
- Criminal exposure in some states (California § 2258: misdemeanor, $500 to $1,000 fine) is rarely enforced against startups but should be stated.
- Contracts generally remain valid, and directors and officers are generally not personally liable for the failure alone, under most states' statutes.
- Practical: banks, payroll providers, landlords, and acquirers ask for good-standing certificates in the operating state.

**How qualification works.** File the state's application (names vary) with (1) the exact Delaware name (or an alternate name if the name is unavailable in that state), (2) a certificate of good standing or existence from Delaware (most states set a maximum age; order the $50 short-form certificate shortly before filing), (3) a registered agent with a street address in the state, (4) principal office and officer information, and (5) the fee. Then: obtain a state tax account, register for payroll withholding and unemployment insurance before the first payroll there, calendar periodic reports, and keep the agent current. When the company leaves a state, file a withdrawal or surrender so periodic fees and taxes stop.

**California specifics worth stating to every client.**
- The $800 minimum franchise tax is owed every year after the first taxable year, even with losses, until the company formally withdraws. The first-year exemption in R&TC § 23153(f) applies to corporations that incorporate or qualify on or after January 1, 2000; it does not exempt income tax on actual California income. A corporation that was doing business in California before it qualified may be treated as having begun its first taxable year earlier.
- The Statement of Information is due within 90 days of qualifying and every year thereafter; the $250 penalty is assessed by the FTB on the Secretary of State's referral, and continued failure can lead to suspension or forfeiture of the right to do business.
- A suspended or forfeited corporation cannot defend itself in court, and its contracts may be voidable; revivor requires filing all returns and paying all amounts.

**Payroll and employment registrations (every state with employees).**
1. **Federal:** EIN; Form 941 quarterly and Form 940 annually; Forms W-2 to employees and the SSA by January 31; Form I-9 for each hire; report each new hire to the state directory within 20 days of hire (42 U.S.C. § 653a(b)), or sooner if the state requires.
2. **State withholding account** (where the state has an income tax) before the first payroll.
3. **State unemployment insurance account:** California requires registration with the EDD within 15 days after the employer pays more than $100 in wages in a calendar quarter (Cal. Unemp. Ins. Code § 1086); other states set their own triggers.
4. **Workers' compensation insurance** (mandatory in nearly every state; Texas permits non-subscription with notice obligations).
5. **State and local paid-leave, disability, and family-leave programs** (for example, California SDI, New York PFL and DBL, Washington PFML, Massachusetts PFML, Colorado FAMLI), and local taxes (for example, San Francisco and New York City).
6. **Notices and postings,** pay-transparency rules, and wage-statement requirements (see references/employment-and-ip.md).
7. **PEOs and employers of record** may run payroll under their own accounts in some states, but the company itself must still qualify where it transacts business.

**Common mistakes.**
- Treating Delaware incorporation as sufficient for a California-based company.
- Missing the California Statement of Information because it is due 90 days after qualifying, then annually.
- Hiring a remote employee in a new state without payroll registration, creating withholding and unemployment-insurance arrears.
- Forgetting to withdraw from a state after the last employee leaves, so minimum taxes and annual reports keep accruing.
- Letting the Delaware franchise tax lapse, which blocks the good-standing certificate every other state requires for qualification.
